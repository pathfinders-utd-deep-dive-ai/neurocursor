\begin{abstract}
NeuroCursor evaluates whether a brief randomized point-and-click challenge can
serve as a supplemental behavioral identity signal. A five-movement decision
takes a mean 4.43 s in the repeated-split evaluation. Each movement is represented
by raw, spatial, kinematic, geometric, and target-relative time-series channels.
We compare a candidate convolutional neural network--gated recurrent unit
(CNN--GRU), CNN-only and GRU-only networks, five classical classifiers, three
primary feature sets, and decisions formed from one, three, or five movements. The
evaluable cohort contains 10 anonymized identities, 266 sessions, and 1,330
movements; sessions are isolated across training, validation, and test subsets,
and user-specific thresholds are calibrated only on validation data. Across 30
fixed session-separated splits, the strict full-feature logistic verifier at
$K=5$ achieves mean pooled balanced accuracy of 82.82\%, mean macro false
acceptance rate (FAR) of 0.63\%, mean macro false rejection rate (FRR) of
35.89\%, and mean macro ROC--AUC of 98.78\%. Only 8 of 30 splits have zero
observed false accepts. GRU-only and several classical baselines outperform the
CNN--GRU on key metrics, while feature ablations show mixed rather than uniform
benefit from higher-order channels. These results support cursor dynamics as a
research direction for step-up authentication, but the small cohort, high FRR,
split sensitivity, absent device metadata, and limited enrollment-size and
sequence-order evidence preclude deployment claims.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Mouse dynamics can supplement credential-based authentication by measuring
how a person moves and clicks \cite{nistBiometrics,khanMouseSurvey}.
Existing engineered-feature, convolutional, recurrent, and hybrid systems
already establish this modality \cite{antalFejer2020,wang2025Mouse}.
The contribution is neither a new biometric modality nor a new CNN--GRU
architecture.

NeuroCursor instead evaluates a brief randomized target-acquisition challenge.
The user moves to and clicks each target; a decision combines
$K\in\{1,3,5\}$ recorded trajectories. Randomization varies direction and
distance while enabling target-relative measurements. We ask whether these
few movements support \emph{supplemental} identity verification.

The cohort contains 10 identities, 266 sessions, and 1,330 movements. We compare
three neural models, five classical classifiers, and three feature sets using
session-separated splits and validation-only threshold calibration. Across
30 splits, strict logistic verification at $K=5$ yields mean macro FAR 0.63\%
and FRR 35.89\%: low observed false acceptance comes with substantial legitimate
user rejection. GRU-only and several classical models outperform CNN--GRU on
key metrics; the hybrid is a tested candidate, not the recommended model.

\paragraph{Research questions and hypotheses}
The experiments ask whether (RQ1) CNN--GRU improves on neural and classical
baselines; (RQ2) combining one, three, or five movements changes the
security--usability tradeoff; (RQ3) engineered and target-relative channels add
value beyond raw channels; (RQ4) validation-calibrated thresholds shift FAR and
FRR; (RQ5) results remain stable across session-separated splits; and (RQ6)
acquisition conditions could confound the measured signal. We test four
directional hypotheses: CNN--GRU will have lower EER than CNN-only and GRU-only
(H1); $K=5$ will improve verification over $K=1$ (H2); the full feature set will
outperform raw channels (H3); and session separation will be more difficult than
random movement-level splitting (H4). Negative and mixed outcomes are reported
without relabeling the hypotheses after inspection.

\paragraph{Contributions}
This work contributes:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item a randomized five-target challenge and a 27-channel,
    target-relative representation for short-form cursor verification;
    \item a session-isolated evaluation that distinguishes movement scores,
    $K$-movement decisions, validation calibration, and held-out testing;
    \item direct neural, classical, interaction-count, feature, threshold, and
    repeated-split comparisons, including negative results; and
    \item a reproducible implementation and a deployment-oriented analysis of
    uncertainty, device confounding, accessibility, and adversarial scope.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

Mouse-dynamics studies differ in collection task, representation, classifier,
and evaluation protocol. Limited public data, inconsistent protocols, and
hardware variation complicate comparison \cite{khanMouseSurvey}.
Antal and Fej\'er used fixed-size Balabit sequences and DFL transfer learning
with a CNN \cite{antalFejer2020}; Antal and Egyed-Zsigmond studied continuous
intrusion detection \cite{antalEgyed2019}. These passive-data protocols differ
from a deliberate target-acquisition challenge.

Recent work addresses complementary weaknesses. Dave \textit{et al.} compare
mouse authentication in high- and low-intensity games, exposing task context
as an evaluation dimension rather than studying a fixed short challenge
\cite{dave2024Clicks}. Kuric \textit{et al.} show that mouse configuration
alters common metrics and downstream predictions \cite{kuricMouseCredibility}.
Wang \textit{et al.}'s LT-AMouse combines 1D-ResNet and GRU with density-based
data-volume analysis and approximate-entropy segment selection on Balabit and
DFL \cite{wang2025Mouse}. Its data-sufficiency and attack analyses are broader
than ours, including segment-length sensitivity and public-dataset coverage. We cite the verified arXiv v2 record; no journal publication is
identified in that record.

Newer architectures broaden both representation and evaluation. Jin and Liao
use trajectory embeddings and a shared classifier for authentication and
CAPTCHA-farm identity-inconsistency detection, reducing the need for separate
per-user classifiers \cite{jin2025Similarity}. Tao \textit{et al.} combine
switching LSTM, mixed depthwise convolution, and personalized isolation
forests, with evaluation on Balabit and SapiMouse \cite{tao2026Spatiotemporal}.
Sasikumar and Nagarajan combine a Transformer--CNN with federated learning,
differential privacy, and robustness tests \cite{sasikumar2026Privacy}.
These studies address scalability, temporal/spatial representation, or privacy
more directly than NeuroCursor, but use different tasks and decision units;
their reported metrics are not head-to-head comparisons with five-target
verification.

Bot detection must also be distinguished from claimed-user verification.
Acien \textit{et al.}'s BeCAPTCHA-Mouse uses neuromotor features and synthetic
trajectories to distinguish people from bots \cite{acien2022BeCAPTCHA}.
It is directly relevant to trajectory modeling and synthetic attacks, but
human-versus-bot accuracy does not establish human identity separation.
Commercial descriptions similarly show operational interest without enough
public detail to reproduce identity thresholds
\cite{graboxyContinuous,graboxyMFA,googleRecaptchaV3}.
Table~\ref{tab:related_work_positioning} summarizes these distinctions.
NeuroCursor contributes a session-isolated, short randomized challenge with
explicit calibration, classical comparators, and reported negative results,
not a new biometric modality or a demonstrated need for CNN--GRU.

\section{NeuroCursor System Design}

\subsection{System Overview}
NeuroCursor consists of four major stages: enrollment, challenge generation,
trajectory recording, and verification. During enrollment, each participant
completes randomized point-and-click tasks used to construct that user's
behavioral profile. During authentication, the system generates new target
locations and records each trajectory as the user selects a target. The
trajectory is preprocessed, represented as a feature sequence, evaluated by a
user-specific verifier, and compared with the claimed user's threshold.

The implementation uses one-versus-rest verification. When identity $u$ is
claimed, trajectories from all other enrolled identities are impostor samples.
Each movement is interpolated to a fixed sequence length and normalized using
training-subset statistics only. The experiments feed these sequences to the
three neural candidates or convert them to fixed-dimensional summaries for the
classical baselines. During authentication, movement scores from one attempt
are averaged and compared with the validation-calibrated threshold for $u$.

\subsection{Randomized Challenge Generation}

Let $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^2$ denote the usable screen region in which the target may appear. For each movement $k$, the system samples a target location

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{q}^{(k)}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
q_x^{(k)}\\
q_y^{(k)}
\end{bmatrix}
\in \Omega.
\label{eq:target_location}
\end{equation}

The browser uses a $1400\times600$ canvas. The participant first selects a
$100\times50$ START control centered at $(700,300)$. Each of the five numbered
targets has size $50\times50$. Target coordinates are sampled independently as

\begin{equation}
q_x^{(k)}\sim\mathcal{U}_{\mathbb{Z}}\{200,\ldots,1200\},
\qquad
q_y^{(k)}\sim\mathcal{U}_{\mathbb{Z}}\{200,\ldots,400\}.
\label{eq:target_sampling_bounds}
\end{equation}

The implementation does not enforce a minimum distance between consecutive
targets. Randomization varies the requested target position but does not
guarantee a balanced distribution of movement directions or magnitudes.

\subsection{Trajectory Recording}

The data-acquisition process uses a browser-based capture loop scheduled at approximately 60 Hz, although the resulting inter-sample intervals are nonuniform because of browser task scheduling and pointer-event delivery. Each discrete event record stores the elapsed time and the absolute coordinates of both the cursor and the intended target. The system also captures the relative position of the cursor and target, the current mouse-button state, and a movement index for sequence tracking. To preserve spatial context, each record includes the target dimensions and the overall canvas dimensions.

Recorded target coordinates are target centers, not upper-left corners, as
used by the acquisition interface's drawing and hit-testing functions.
During each movement, NeuroCursor records cursor coordinates and their respective timestamps. A raw trajectory is represented as

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{R}
=
\left\{
(x_i,y_i,t_i)
\right\}_{i=1}^{T}.
\label{eq:raw_trajectory}
\end{equation}

From this raw trajectory, the system computes derived temporal and kinematic variables. For instance, the elapsed time between sequential samples is

\begin{equation}
\Delta t_i = t_i - t_{i-1}.
\label{eq:delta_t}
\end{equation}
The displacement vector is
\begin{equation}
\Delta \mathbf{r}_i
=
\mathbf{r}_i-\mathbf{r}_{i-1},
\label{eq:displacement}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{r}_i=[x_i,y_i]^{\top}$. Velocity and acceleration can be
approximated by
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{v}_i
=
\frac{\Delta \mathbf{r}_i}{\Delta t_i},
\qquad
\mathbf{a}_i
=
\frac{\mathbf{v}_i-\mathbf{v}_{i-1}}{\Delta t_i}.
\label{eq:discrete_velocity_acceleration}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Authentication Workflow}

For each claimed user $u$, NeuroCursor receives a challenge sequence $\mathcal{Q}$ and outputs an authentication score $S(\mathcal{Q},u)$. The decision rule is controlled by threshold $\tau$. If $S(\mathcal{Q},u)\geq \tau$, the attempt is accepted; otherwise, it is rejected. In a real deployment, rejection would not necessarily imply that access is denied permanently. Rather, it could trigger a secondary authentication process.

\section{Feature Engineering}

\subsection{Spatial and Temporal Features}

Spatial and temporal features describe cursor geometry and timing: duration,
path length, straight-line distance, mean and maximum speed, mean acceleration
magnitude, pause duration, and click timing. Total movement duration is

\begin{equation}
D = t_T - t_1.
\label{eq:movement_duration}
\end{equation}

The straight-line distance from the initial cursor position to the target is

\begin{equation}
D_{\mathrm{straight}}
=
\left\|
\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{r}_1
\right\|_2.
\label{eq:straight_distance}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Kinematic Features}

Kinematic features describe how movement unfolds over time. Speed at sample $i$ can be computed as

\begin{equation}
v_i
=
\left\|
\mathbf{v}_i
\right\|_2,
\label{eq:speed}
\end{equation}

and acceleration magnitude can be computed as

\begin{equation}
a_i
=
\left\|
\mathbf{a}_i
\right\|_2.
\label{eq:acceleration_magnitude}
\end{equation}

Jerk can be approximated as
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{j}_i
=
\frac{\mathbf{a}_i-\mathbf{a}_{i-1}}{\Delta t_i}.
\label{eq:jerk}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Higher-Order Differential Features}

Since cursor events may be recorded at nonuniform time intervals, kinematic quantities are estimated with respect to the observed timestamps. For the planar curve trajectory $\mathbf{r}(t)$, jerk is the third temporal derivative:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{j}(t)
=
\frac{d^3\mathbf{r}(t)}{dt^3}.
\label{eq:continuous_jerk}
\end{equation}

The signed curvature of the trajectory is

\begin{equation}
\kappa(t)
=
\frac{
x'(t)y''(t)-y'(t)x''(t)
}{
\left(x'(t)^2+y'(t)^2\right)^{3/2}
}.
\label{eq:signed_curvature}
\end{equation}



Movement intensity and smoothness may be summarized through acceleration
and jerk energy:

\begin{equation}
E_a
=
\int_{t_0}^{t_f}
\|\mathbf{a}(t)\|_2^2\,dt,
\qquad
E_j
=
\int_{t_0}^{t_f}
\|\mathbf{j}(t)\|_2^2\,dt.
\label{eq:acceleration_jerk_energy}
\end{equation}

A duration- and distance-adjusted smoothness measure is the dimensionless
jerk:

\begin{equation}
J^{*}
=
\frac{D^5}{\widehat{L}^{\,2}}
\int_{t_0}^{t_f}
\|\mathbf{j}(t)\|_2^2\,dt,
\label{eq:dimensionless_jerk}
\end{equation}

where $D$ is the duration of the movement and $\widehat{L}$ is the discrete path
length. Larger values indicate less smooth movement.

Derivatives use recorded timestamps and unsmoothed, canvas-normalized
coordinates. Negative timestamp differences are rejected; zero or negligible
positive intervals are replaced by $10^{-8}$ s. This numerical safeguard does
not remove differentiation noise.

\subsection{Target-Relative Features}

Because NeuroCursor uses randomized target locations, target-relative features are important. These features describe cursor motion with respect to the current target rather than only in terms of screen coordinates. The target-relative displacement at sample $i$ is

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{d}_i
=
\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{r}_i.
\label{eq:target_relative_displacement}
\end{equation}

The target distance is

\begin{equation}
d_i
=
\left\|
\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{r}_i
\right\|_2.
\label{eq:target_distance}
\end{equation}

The target-closure rate can be approximated as

\begin{equation}
c_i
=
\frac{d_{i-1}-d_i}{\Delta t_i}.
\label{eq:closure_rate}
\end{equation}

Positive values indicate movement toward the target, while negative values
may indicate overshoot or corrective motion.

\subsection{Normalization and Sequence Preparation}

The implementation first normalizes cursor and target coordinates using the
recorded canvas width and height:

\begin{equation}
x_i'=\frac{x_i}{W},
\qquad
y_i'=\frac{y_i}{H},
\label{eq:spatial_normalization}
\end{equation}

where $W$ and $H$ denote canvas dimensions ($1400\times600$ for this cohort),
not independently measured physical display dimensions. Displacements,
velocities, accelerations, jerk, and target-relative quantities are computed
from these normalized coordinates; elapsed time is expressed in seconds.
Because width and height differ, this anisotropic scaling changes angles,
curvature, angular velocity, heading error, and cross-track geometry relative
to pixel-space or isotropic coordinates. These are dimensionless canvas-space
features, not physical motor measurements; an isotropic-normalization control
was not conducted.
The feature-extraction equations use this normalized coordinate convention in
the implementation. After interpolation, all selected sequence channels are
standardized using training-set means and standard deviations:

\begin{equation}
z
=
\frac{x-\mu_{\mathrm{train}}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{train}}}.
\label{eq:zscore_normalization}
\end{equation}

Standard deviations below $10^{-7}$ are replaced by one to avoid division by
near-zero values. Classical summary vectors are standardized separately using
only their training subset.

Trajectories consist of different numbers of recorded cursor samples because movement duration and browser event frequency vary between interactions. NeuroCursor handles such variation by linearly interpolating each feature channel to a fixed sequence length of $T=128$ steps. The same interpolation process is applied to training, validation, testing, and authentication samples. Padding, masking, and variable-length recurrent processing are not used in the final implemented model.

The interpolation can be represented as

\begin{equation}
\widetilde{x}_{j,c}
=
\operatorname{Interp}
\left(
\{(s_i,x_{i,c})\}_{i=1}^{T_{\mathrm{raw}}},
\widetilde{s}_j
\right),
\label{eq:sequence_interpolation}
\end{equation}

where $s_i\in[0,1]$ denotes the normalized position of the original sample,
$\widetilde{s}_j\in[0,1]$ denotes the position of the interpolated sample,
and $c$ indexes the feature channel.

\subsection{Feature Semantics and Sources of Variation}

Coordinates describe trajectory location and shape; spatial and kinematic
channels describe progress and motion; target-relative channels describe
approach to the requested destination. None guarantees invariance to task
geometry or acquisition conditions. Differentiation without smoothing can
amplify quantization and irregular event timing. Interpolation preserves
sample order but can alter brief patterns and does not create independent
observations. Because elapsed and inter-event times remain explicit channels,
resampling does not remove timing information. Predictive features therefore
need not reflect stable physiological traits; the ablations test predictive
utility, not causality.

\section{Candidate Neural Architectures}

The input to the neural model is a multivariate sequence $\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times d}$, where $T$ is the number of time steps and $d$ is the number of feature channels. Each row corresponds to one time step, and each column corresponds to one recorded or derived feature.

The complete sequence representation contains normalized cursor coordinates, elapsed time, inter-sample time, button state, displacement, horizontal and vertical velocity, speed magnitude, horizontal and vertical acceleration, acceleration magnitude, horizontal and vertical jerk, jerk magnitude, heading, angular velocity, curvature, cumulative path progress, running path efficiency, target-relative displacement, target distance, target-closure rate, target-heading error, and cross-track error.

timing, acceleration energy, jerk energy, and dimensionless jerk. Consequently,
the full neural and classical inputs contain the same source channels but are
not the same mathematical representation.

The implementation computes these additional movement summaries after channel
interpolation to 128 steps. Path length sums the resampled displacement
magnitudes; acceleration/jerk energies sum squared resampled magnitudes
weighted by resampled inter-event intervals. These discrete estimators need
not equal quantities recomputed directly from the raw event stream:
interpolated displacement and interval channels are not necessarily
conservative under summation. Their names describe implemented estimators,
not exact recovery of physical path length or continuous-time energy. This
also leaves sensitivity to original event density and acquisition conditions.

For reproducibility, pause duration is the sum of resampled inter-event
intervals whose associated speed is at or below that movement's 10th-percentile
resampled speed; it is a relative low-speed proxy, not a fixed physical-speed
or inactivity threshold. Click timing is the elapsed time at the first
resampled positive button-state value, or the movement duration if none is
positive. Button state is interpolated with the other channels, so this
estimator can precede the original recorded press. It measures neither
press-to-release duration nor reaction latency from target appearance.
Summary path efficiency divides the initial target distance by the resampled
path-length estimator (zero when that length is negligible); unlike the
running-efficiency channel, this summary is not clipped to $[0,1]$.

\subsection{Implemented Network Configuration}

Table~\ref{tab:model_architecture} specifies the implemented CNN--GRU.
It receives an input tensor of dimensions $128\times d$,
where $d$ is the number of selected feature channels. The first temporal
convolution contains 32 filters with a kernel length of five, same padding, and
ReLU activation. It is followed by batch normalization, max pooling with a pool
size of two, and dropout with probability $0.20$. The second convolution
contains 64 filters with a kernel length of three, same padding, and ReLU
activation, followed by batch normalization and a second max-pooling operation.
The two pooling operations reduce 128 time steps to 32; the CNN therefore emits
an ordered $32\times64$ representation into the GRU, not a single 64-value
feature vector.

\subsection{Neural Baselines and Output}

The CNN--GRU feeds the convolutional sequence to a 64-unit GRU with dropout
0.20. All neural models use the same 32-unit ReLU dense head, dropout 0.30,
and a one-unit sigmoid output. CNN-only replaces the GRU with global average
pooling; GRU-only applies the same GRU directly to the input sequence.
These are component-removal baselines, not parameter-matched architectures.
The sigmoid output is a verifier score, not a demonstrated calibrated
probability of genuine identity.

\subsection{Training Objective}

For binary verification, the implemented model is trained using binary cross-entropy:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}
=
-
\frac{1}{N}
\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\left[
y_i\log(\hat{y}_i)
+
(1-y_i)\log(1-\hat{y}_i)
\right],
\label{eq:bce_loss}
\end{equation}

where $y_i$ is the true label and $\hat{y}_i$ is the predicted probability.

\subsection{Training Configuration}

The model is optimized using Adam with an initial learning rate of $10^{-3}$. Binary cross-entropy is used as the optimization objective. Training is conducted for a maximum of 50 epochs with a batch size of 32. Early stopping monitors validation loss with a patience of eight epochs and restores the parameters associated with the lowest observed validation loss. The best model checkpoint is stored separately for each enrolled user.

Since each one-versus-rest dataset consists of substantially more impostor samples than genuine samples, class weights are calculated inversely to class frequency. For class $c$, the weight is

\begin{equation}
w_c
=
\frac{N}{2N_c},
\label{eq:class_weight}
\end{equation}

where $N$ is the total number of training samples and $N_c$ is the number
belonging to class $c$.

\section{Experimental Methodology}

\subsection{Dataset Construction}

The four authors recruited participants and collected 951 sessions from July 1
to July 31, 2026, through an explanatory URL shared online and in room 2.415 of
the ECSS building at The University of Texas at Dallas. Participants were
informed about the research and intended publication and consented to
participation. Of these sessions from 49 identity labels, 683 used a legacy schema
without required target/movement fields. The full-schema subset contained
268 sessions from 11 identities. Excluding one identity with only two sessions
left 10 identities, 266 sessions, and 1,330 movements
(Table~\ref{tab:cohort_construction}). Each eligible session is one attempt
containing five chronologically ordered movements. Eligibility used schema
completeness and session counts, not model performance.

Integrity checks found no duplicate composite movement identifiers, exact
duplicate samples, missing session/attempt identifiers, or session overlap
across subsets. Records use anonymized identity and session labels.
Hardware, pointing-device, DPI, sensitivity, and browser labels
were unavailable.

For each claimed user, genuine samples are formed from that specific user's authentication attempts. Impostor samples are obtained by comparing attempts from other users against the claimed user's biometric profile. This produces a binary verification dataset:

\begin{equation}
y_i
=
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{genuine attempt},\\
0, & \text{impostor attempt}.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:binary_labels}
\end{equation}

NeuroCursor trains a separate binary verification model for each enrolled user. For a model associated with a specific user $u$, movements from $u$ are assigned the genuine label $y=1$, whereas movements from every other participant are assigned the impostor label $y=0$. Consequently, a system containing $U$ enrolled users contains $U$ user-specific binary classifiers and decision thresholds.
The main evaluation therefore tests new sessions from known impostor identities:
test impostors also contribute training negatives and validation observations.
It does not estimate rejection of previously unseen people. A separate
identity-exclusion control is reported in Section~\ref{sec:unseen_control}.

\subsection{Training, Validation, and Test Splits}

For each identity, roughly 75\% of sessions are allocated to training,
15\% to validation, and 10\% to testing; every movement from a session stays
together. Seed 42 defines the fixed model comparison. The complete classical
pipeline is refitted for each seed from 0 to 29 to measure split sensitivity
on the same cohort, not external replication. Random movement-level splitting
is used only in the secondary split-comparison baseline; it did not improve
the reported metrics here.

\subsection{Threshold Calibration}

The operating threshold for each claimed user is selected exclusively using the validation set. In the fixed model-comparison configuration, the threshold is chosen at the validation operating point at which the false acceptance rate and false rejection rate are approximately equal. After calibration, the threshold is frozen and applied without modification to test scores. Within each fit, test labels are not used for preprocessing, parameter updates, early stopping, or threshold calibration. This procedural isolation does not make the overall study an untouched confirmation: earlier test comparisons informed subsequent development analyses, as disclosed below.

We evaluated a second, security-focused policy in a separate analysis. For each claimed identity, we defined the strict threshold as the lowest validation-set threshold that resulted in zero observed false accepts; when multiple thresholds met this criterion, we selected the one with the lower false rejection rate. The threshold was then frozen before test evaluation. Achieving zero observed false accepts on the validation set is a finite-sample calibration rule and should not be interpreted as a guarantee of zero false accepts in a larger population.

The validation-EER policy is the fixed standard for the seed-42 model comparison.
The primary repeated-split performance estimate instead uses the strict logistic
policy. These are distinct analyses, not interchangeable operating points.

\subsection{Decision Construction and Evaluation Isolation}

A movement is one point-and-click trajectory; each eligible session contains
one five-movement attempt. The first $K$ chronological valid movements form
an attempt score (Eq.~\ref{eq:aggregate_score}); scores never determine which
movements are selected. Each physical attempt contributes one genuine
comparison and $U-1$ impostor comparisons across $U$ verifiers, producing a
9:1 impostor/genuine decision ratio here.

Unless explicitly labeled pooled, table metrics (including model-comparison
accuracy) average verifiers equally. Pooled metrics use summed confusion
counts. All movements from a session stay together. Within each fit, training
data determine preprocessing and parameters, validation data determine early
stopping and thresholds, and the frozen pipeline is evaluated on test data.
The full pipeline is refitted for every sensitivity split. Reused participants
and test-informed development make these exploratory partition-sensitivity
analyses, not independent confirmation.

\subsection{Interaction-Count Evaluation}

For interaction-count experiments, chronologically ordered movement scores from the same authentication attempt are aggregated into subsets of $K\in\{1,3,5\}$. The attempt score is the arithmetic mean of the first $K$ valid movement scores,

\begin{equation}
\bar{s}_{u,K}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}s_{u,k},
\label{eq:aggregate_score}
\end{equation}

where $s_{u,k}$ is movement $k$'s score for claimed user $u$. FAR, FRR,
EER, ROC--AUC, and average duration are calculated separately for each $K$.
Only attempts containing at least $K$ valid movements are included.

\subsection{Enrollment-Size and Temporal-Dependence Scope}
\label{sec:scope_controls}

The eligibility rule of at least three sessions per identity exists only to
permit disjoint training, validation, and test subsets. It is not evidence that
three sessions are enough for stable enrollment. Exact-budget learning
curves evaluate logistic regression, CNN--GRU, and GRU-only across session splits
0, 1, and 2. Eligibility is determined before fitting: the seven identities
with at least 27 complete sessions can each supply 20 training sessions after
the validation/test holdouts. This cohort contains 217 sessions and 1,085
movements; each test attempt supplies one genuine and six impostor comparisons.
This fixed cohort is used for every budget.
Within each split, validation and test sessions remain unchanged. A fixed
random ordering of each identity's training sessions defines nested budgets
of exactly 1, 5, 10, 20, and all available sessions per identity.
Each session supplies five movements. Both
genuine and impostor training pools change, so this tests cohort-wide training
scale rather than isolating genuine enrollment from background-data quantity.
The seven-identity results are not directly comparable to ten-identity primary
results. All preprocessing and thresholds are refitted using only the selected
training and unchanged validation subsets, respectively.

Before inspecting this experiment grid, we specified a study-specific
criterion for a usable stable budget: strict FAR at most 1\% and FRR at most
10\% in every split, with changes no larger than 1 percentage point FAR and
5 percentage points FRR at every larger tested budget. This is an exploratory
operational definition, not a population guarantee or an external standard.

To test chronological structure without explicit time or derivative cues,
both neural architectures receive only normalized $x,y$ coordinates. Elapsed
time, inter-event time, button state, derivatives, path progress, and
target-relative channels are excluded. Ordered, independently time-permuted,
and reversed $128\times2$ inputs are compared across splits 0, 1, and 2 using
all ten identities. Transformations occur after interpolation in all three
partitions. Joint permutation preserves coordinate pairs and exact channel
marginals; reversal preserves the same distributions. Within each architecture,
input dimensions, parameter counts, and training settings are unchanged.
A logistic comparator receives the eight order-invariant coordinate summaries.
These controls test order sensitivity, not mutual information. Path geometry
may still convey plausible trajectory structure even when explicit time cues
are absent. Sample density and successive position differences can also
encode motion and event-delivery information. The control removes explicit
timing and derivative channels; it does not eliminate every implicit
kinematic or acquisition cue.

The classical feature-quality control is repeated across the same three
session-split seeds (0, 1, and 2), yielding six configurations.
Both full and low-order inputs use
only mean, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum per channel. Neither arm
receives the 13 extra derived statistics. Removing acceleration, jerk, angular
velocity, and curvature therefore changes 108 summaries to 76 while preserving
the same summary rule for every retained channel. Deterministic TensorFlow
operations are enabled, intra- and inter-operation parallelism are each set
to one thread, and the random seed is reset before each condition.
The experiment grid contains 72 configurations;
all outcomes and actual session counts are archived. These are retrospective
development experiments on reused data, not independent confirmation.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

The mouse-dynamics verification framework is evaluated using accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, ROC--AUC, false acceptance rate, false rejection rate, and equal error rate.

Accuracy is defined as

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Accuracy}
=
\frac{TP+TN}{TP+TN+FP+FN}.
\label{eq:accuracy}
\end{equation}

Because each ten-user test attempt yields one genuine comparison and nine
impostor comparisons, ordinary accuracy is affected by a 9:1 decision
imbalance. Balanced accuracy is therefore also reported:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{BalancedAccuracy}
=
\frac{1}{2}
\left(
\frac{TP}{TP+FN}
+
\frac{TN}{TN+FP}
\right).
\label{eq:balanced_accuracy}
\end{equation}

Macro metrics are calculated per user-specific verifier and then averaged
equally across users. Pooled metrics are recomputed from summed confusion
counts. These values are reported separately because they use different
aggregation weights.

False acceptance rate is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{FAR}
=
\frac{FP}{FP+TN}.
\label{eq:far}
\end{equation}

False rejection rate is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{FRR}
=
\frac{FN}{FN+TP}.
\label{eq:frr}
\end{equation}

The implementation approximates equal error rate on the discrete ROC
threshold grid $\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{ROC}}$ by selecting the closest FAR/FRR
pair and averaging the two rates:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\tau^* &= \arg\min_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{ROC}}}
|\mathrm{FAR}(\tau)-\mathrm{FRR}(\tau)|,\\
\widehat{\mathrm{EER}} &=
\frac{\mathrm{FAR}(\tau^*)+\mathrm{FRR}(\tau^*)}{2}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:eer}
\end{equation}

Interpretation of ROC curves and EER follows established ROC-analysis and biometric performance-reporting guidance \cite{fawcettROC,isoBiometricTesting}.
Reported EER is computed from the test-set ROC as a descriptive ranking
summary; its threshold is not used for operational test predictions.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}

In the fixed seed-42 CNN--GRU analysis, we compare genuine and impostor scores for each user using a one-sided permutation test of the hypothesis $\mathbb{E}[S_G]>\mathbb{E}[S_I]$. Cohen's $d$ quantifies the degree of separation \cite{cohenPower}. For performance intervals, we use class-stratified bootstrap resampling for ROC--AUC \cite{efronBootstrap} and exact binomial Clopper--Pearson limits \cite{clopperPearson} for FAR and FRR. We interpret statistical significance in conjunction with observed effect sizes and biometric error rates. The distribution across the 30 repeated splits is summarized using the mean, median, interquartile range, minimum, and maximum. Because these splits repeatedly use the same user cohort, we do not assign an independence-based confidence interval to these results.

These interval and permutation calculations are conditional, descriptive
summaries, not cluster-adjusted population inference. Binomial coverage
requires independent Bernoulli trials with a common error probability.
Here, each physical attempt is reused across verifiers, and sessions from the
same participant can remain dependent. Pooling decisions therefore does not
create independent trials or establish a common population error probability.
The bootstrap and permutation procedures also do not model participant-level
clustering. A confirmatory study needs a prespecified participant/session-aware
analysis; the present calculations do not resolve that dependence.
Table~\ref{tab:descriptive_statistics} reports the archived per-verifier results.
No multiple-testing correction is applied, and these tests are not treated as
confirmatory evidence of population-level significance.

\subsection{Reproducibility}

For the direct model evaluations, movement-count studies, feature-ablation tests, and split comparisons, we used a pseudorandom seed of 42 to ensure consistency. The robustness analysis reported results for every seed in a fixed grid from 0 to 29, with the verifier refitted for each split. This grid was established internally for the sensitivity analysis and was not externally preregistered. Preprocessing statistics were derived exclusively from the training subset and archived as part of the experimental record. These artifacts document feature names, sequence lengths, split assignments, training means, standard deviations, model configurations, and user-specific thresholds. The record also includes confusion counts and the resulting performance metrics. For the primary neural evaluation, the software environment consisted of TensorFlow 2.16.2, scikit-learn 1.6.1, and NumPy 1.26.4 running on an ARM64 CPU architecture.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Interpretation of Results}

The 30-split distribution is more informative than the favorable seed-42
snapshot. High mean ROC--AUC (98.78\%) coexists with mean FRR 35.89\%
(range 13.33--58.33\%), indicating strong ranking but an unstable strict
operating point. This supports further study of supplemental verification,
not standalone deployment.

The results do not justify choosing CNN--GRU over GRU-only or the strongest
classical models. Redundant local filters and greater estimation difficulty
are plausible explanations, not established causes. Learning curves and
feature-removal controls demonstrate sensitivity to data and representation;
order controls hold capacity fixed within architecture but do not isolate
between-architecture capacity or optimization effects. Mixed and negative
outcomes are retained rather than selecting a favorable model or split.

\subsection{Security, Usability, and External Validation}

The evaluated impostors are other enrolled participants, not deliberate
attackers. Replay, synthetic event injection, informed imitation, and browser
manipulation were not tested. No resistance to those attacks is claimed.
A low behavioral score should therefore trigger an established step-up factor,
not irreversible denial of access \cite{nistBiometrics,deutschmannContinuousAuthentication}.

More movements improved repeated strict-verifier averages but increased mean
challenge duration from 0.98 s at $K=1$ to 4.43 s at $K=5$.
Thresholds must balance false acceptance, false rejection, accessibility, and
recovery costs. A pilot should measure acquisition/enrollment failures, retries,
completion time, and participant/device-specific errors; authenticate template
updates independently and provide a conventional recovery path.

Confirmation requires an untouched external cohort and a preregistered
sampling and analysis plan \cite{isoBiometricTesting}. Collect longitudinal,
cross-device/browser sessions with acquisition metadata and dedicated replay,
injection, and imitation trials. Freeze models and thresholds before testing,
and report user/session-aware uncertainty rather than treating reused verifier
decisions as independent trials. Rare-event FAR claims need substantially more
impostor trials than this study provides. Privacy, template protection, and
accessibility must be evaluated alongside classifier performance.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

This investigation is subject to several critical limitations. The evaluable
cohort is restricted to 10 anonymized identities and 266 sessions; one identity
contributed only three eligible sessions, making some validation and test rates
highly discrete. Thirty repeated splits reuse this same cohort and measure
partition sensitivity, not population generalization. The exact-budget
enrollment curves cover three splits and all three model families, but restrict
the cohort to seven identities with sufficient history. They do not characterize
data-poor identities or establish a population minimum collection duration.
Their explicit error/stability rule is study-specific, and the few genuine
test attempts make error rates coarse. The three-session inclusion rule in
the primary study remains a splitting requirement only.

Coordinate-only permutation and reversal controls cover three splits and hold
capacity fixed within each architecture. They show no consistent chronological
advantage and do not measure mutual information. Even coordinate geometry can
contain plausible trajectory structure. Between-architecture capacity,
optimization behavior, and broader feature interactions remain unresolved.
The common-summary classical ablation isolates the channel-group change from
removal of the 13 derived summaries, but correlated predictors still prevent
causal attribution to an individual derivative.

Recruitment context is author-reported rather than encoded per session.
Hardware, input-device type (mouse versus trackpad),
DPI, sensitivity, browser, handedness, fatigue, injury, and environmental
labels were unavailable, preventing cross-device analysis and leaving
acquisition confounding unresolved. The dataset contains no replay or deliberate
imitation attacks, and its impostor sample is insufficient for deployment-grade
rare-event FAR claims. Finally, multiple seed-42 configurations and thresholds
were inspected during report preparation, so a preregistered external cohort is
required for confirmatory evaluation.

\subsection{Ethical and Privacy Considerations}

Behavioral biometrics should be treated as a sensitive modality because they may reflect distinctive patterns of user interaction. A practical system should minimize unnecessary data collection, store only the data required for authentication, protect stored templates, and provide clear notice to users.

The authors report receiving verbal guidance from Hamid Rouhani after his
review of institutional guidelines and the acquisition code and consultation
with his supervisor. No written UTD OHSP/IRB determination or exemption has
been provided for this manuscript; verbal guidance is not presented as formal
IRB approval. Written institutional clarification remains necessary before
submission. Participants were informed about the research and intended
publication and consented to participation. Collection occurred online and in
UTD ECSS room 2.415 during July 1--31, 2026. The instrument records cursor
coordinates, timestamps, and button state in a bounded canvas, using anonymized
labels. Anonymization does not by itself establish exemption, particularly
because the analysis seeks to distinguish individuals from their behavior.

\section{Conclusion}

NeuroCursor provides a randomized five-movement challenge with a mean completion
time of 4.43 s and mean ROC--AUC of 98.78\% across 30 session-separated splits.
It evaluates cursor behavior as a supplemental identity signal. Strict full-feature
logistic verification at $K=5$ achieved mean pooled balanced accuracy 82.82\%,
macro FAR 0.63\%, macro FRR 35.89\%, and macro ROC--AUC 98.78\%.
High FRR and split variability preclude deployment claims. CNN--GRU did not
outperform GRU-only or the strongest classical models and is not recommended
on this evidence.

The controlled experiments show training-scale sensitivity but no stable enrollment
minimum under the stated criterion; coordinate-only order controls show no
consistent chronological advantage or mutual-information evidence. Removing
higher-order channels did not improve the common-summary classical ablation's mean
metrics. These negative results constrain the interpretation rather than
justify a preferred architecture. An untouched external cohort, longitudinal
and cross-device measurements, dependence-aware uncertainty, and deliberate
attack trials are needed before operational use.
